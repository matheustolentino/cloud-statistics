\section{Experimental site and Instrumentation}\label{sec:site_instruments_products}

The AGORA ACTRIS-CCRES station (37.16$^{\circ}$N, 3.61$^{\circ}$W, 680 m a.s.l) is located in the South of the Iberian Peninsula being one the most meridional CLOUDNET stations. This station has been part of Cloudnet since 2018 and was integrated into ACTRIS CCRES (where ACTRIS stands for Aerosols, Clouds, and Trace gasses Research InfraStructure, and CCRES for Centre for Cloud Remote Sensing) in 2023 as a new national facility. The region experiences a continental climate, with colder months in winter and spring, and warmer months in summer and fall. Particularly, between summer and winter, the temperature and humidity presented the largest differences \citep{bedoya-velasquezSeasonalAnalysisAtmosphere2019}.
The relative humidity below 5 km a.g.l ranges between 60-75\% during winter, and 40-50\% during summer. 
These thermodynamic conditions will be particularly important to understand diurnal cycle of cloud types and inter-annual differences in cloud properties analyzed in further sections.

\subsubsection{Instrumentation}

In this study we use a synergistic combination of a MicroWave Radiometer (MWR), a ceilometer and a 94 GHz Cloud Doppler Radar (CDR). These instruments have been widely used for many Cloudnet stations and shows a promising configuration for long-term cloud observations \citep{illingworthCloudnet2007}, especially to calculate cloud microphysics with better accuracy than satellites \citep{protatAssessmentCloudsatReflectivity2009}. These instruments are operated in AGORA and their relevant technical specifications are shown in table \ref{tab:info_instrument}. This table details operational frequencies or wavelengths, type of measurements, products, measurement ranges, data collection intervals, and references for more details.

The RPG-HATPRO G2 (Humidity and Temperature PROfiler) MWR is a passive remote sensing instrument that measures the brightness temperature (T$_{B}$) around the water vapor (22-31 GHz) and oxygen (51-58 GHz) absorption bands. The instrument is used to retrieve liquid water path (LWP) and integrated water vapor (IWV) (see Tab. \ref{tab:info_instrument}). Their principal characteristics are radiometric accuracy of 0.3-0.4\,K and integration time of 1\,s. More details can be found in \cite{navas-guzmanTroposphericWaterVapour2014a, roseNetworkSuitableMicrowave2005}.

The CHM15k Nimbus ceilometer is an active remote sensing instrument that utilizes a vertically pointed Nd:YAG pulsed laser to measure backscattered photons. The instrument is used to retrieve the attenuated backscattering coefficient ($\beta$) of aerosols and small cloud droplets. Their principal characteristics are repetition frequency intervals of 5-7 kHz, where each pulse is emitted at 1064 nm with 8.4 $\mu$J of energy. The laser beam divergence is 0.3 mrad, and range and time resolution of 15\,m and 15\,s, respectively. Also, the overlap completeness at 1500m a.g.l \citep{heeseCeilometerLidarComparison2010}. More details can be found in \citep{cazorlaRealtimeProcessingCeilometer2017}.

The dual polarimetric RPG-FMCW 94 GHz CDR is also an active remote sensing instrument that measures the Doppler Velocity Spectrum (DVS) for horizontal and vertical linear polarizations at 94 GHz. The instrument is used to compute the reflectivity (Z), mean Doppler velocity (ν$_{D}$), and spectral width (S$_{\omega}$). The CDR also derives the linear depolarization ratio (LDR), enabling the detection of the melting layer, aerosols, and insects as described by \cite{hoganFacilitatingCloudRadar2004}. Their principal characteristics are the frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) signal, which allows different range and time configurations (see Tab. \ref{tab:info_instrument}), the frequency range of 300-3700 kHz and, the antenna beam width of 0.56~$^{\circ}$.

The Cloudnet processing chain \citep{illingworthCloudnet2007} performs extensive quality assurance on raw instruments for providing high-level products, such as target classification, liquid water path, and droplet effective radius. This chain standardizes data processing within the cloud remote sensing community. Their products are derived using a synergistic approach, integrating vertically pointing measurements from ground-based CDRs, MWRs, and ceilometers. Specifically, the target classification product, generated through these three instruments, has been utilized in numerous studies for cloud typing and multi-year analysis \citep{pirloagaGroundBasedMeasurementsCloud2022, nomokonovaStatisticsCloudsTheir2019, kneifelMultiyearCloudPrecipitation2022}, as already commented.

We used a large dataset covering the years 2018 to 2023. Figure \ref{fig:data_availability} illustrates the monthly Cloudnet high level products availability for the AGORA ACTRIS-CCRES station. The availability of this data depends on simultaneous vertically pointing measurements of the RPG-HATPRO G2, CHM15k Nimbus ceilometer, RPG-FMCW 94 GHz CDR, as well as meteorological model products from the ECMWF (European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts) at our station. As can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:data_availability}, the months that less meet these conditions are Jan-Mar, with 40-50\% of the data available, whereas from Apr to Oct the data availability is over 60\%. The years 2018 (270,000 profiles) and 2020 (968,769 profiles) have the least and largest amount of data, respectively. The white bars denote periods with missing data due to instrument maintenance, technical issues, and scanning measurements (which are not processed by Cloudnet). It is worthy to note that most of the missing data are days without clouds, as will be clear in section \ref{sec:anual_variability}. Thus, the recorded dataset shows a solid database, with enough samples to perform an statistical analysis.

\subsubsection{Cloudnet products}\label{sec:cloudnet_products}

The relevant Cloudnet products in this study include only target classification  \citep{hoganFacilitatingCloudRadar2004} and microphysical retrievals, such as effective radius, and liquid and ice water content. A resumed description of microphysical products assumptions and uncertainties are shown in table \ref{tab:microphys}. These products are re-gridded to time resolution of 30\,s and same radar range resolution. Nevertheless, these products can be highly affected by uncorrected radar reflectivity values. For high-frequency radars, such as the RPG models, this variable suffers a strong attenuation by liquid water and gases \citep{hoganAbsoluteCalibration942003}. Hence, Cloudnet applies the two-way gas and liquid attenuation correction for the reflectivity. 

The target classification product (TC) assigns an integer (0-10) to atmospheric particles by height and time. The particle names and its respective numbers are the following: Clear sky (0), Droplets (1), Drizzle or rain (2), Drizzle \& droplets (3), Ice (4), Ice \& droplets (5), Melting ice (6), Melting \& droplets (7), Aerosol (8), Insects (9), Aerosol \& insect (10). Nevertheless, we are interested only in hydrometeors, thus we will refer to HC (hydrometeor classification) as only pixels classified as hydrometeors i.e. from 1 to 7. Briefly, these pixels are classified as follow: Liquid and supercooled droplets were identified using lidar attenuated backscatter and CDR Doppler velocity spectra \citep{schimmelIdentifyingCloudDroplets2022b}, applied only at temperatures above -40$^{\circ}$C. Rain was identified as falling liquid particles below the cloud base, while ice was attributed to particles with wet-bulb temperatures below freezing. The melting layer was detected using wet-bulb temperature divergence and LDR from CDR.

For the AGORA station, many cases of hydrometeor misclassification on HC were observed. Figure \ref{fig:cloudnet_misclassification}.a shows a thin and lower precipitable ice cloud between 08:00 and 20:00. However, at this time, there are just insects and aerosols in the atmosphere, as can be checked by small values of attenuated backscattering (Fig. \ref{fig:cloudnet_misclassification}.b) \comment{and CDR reflectivity}{remove cloud classification and radar reflectivity} (Fig. \ref{fig:cloudnet_misclassification}.c). This occurs because insect detection is highly dependent on the model temperature and the LDR \citep{roschkeDiscriminatingDrizzleRain2024}. Due to the absence of LDR in many stored CDR data files at our station, insect detection is suboptimal in these cases. However, these cases were easily identified and filtered as follow: 
Precipitating and non precipitating ice clouds which were identified with mean cloud base below 4 km (above mean sea level) and average cloud thickness below 700 meters were reclassified as noise. These thresholds were empirically determined by analyzing many different days of target classification products at the Granada station.

Microphysical properties are retrieved in hydrometeor pixels classified by HC. Liquid water content (LWC) and droplet effective radius ($r_{liq}$) are retrieved at “Droplets”, “Drizzle \& droplets” and “Ice \& droplets” pixels. Whereas, Ice water content (IWC) and ice effective radius ($r_{ice}$) are retrieved only in Ice pixels. Table \ref{tab:microphys} shows inputs and output relative error for each microphysical propertie. For LWC, LWP from MWR, temperature (T$_{M}$) and pressure (P$_{M}$) from ECMWF model were used as input parameters. The output has 15\% of relative uncertainty \citep{frischCloudRadarMicrowave1998}.
For r$_{liq}$, reflectivity (Z) from CDR and default values of lognormal droplet size distribution (DSD) were used as input. The output has 15\% of uncertainty due to Z errors \citep{frischRetrievalStratusCloud2002}. 
IWC is retrieved troughout the Z-IWC-T relation \citep{hoganRetrievalIceWater2006}, where Z and T$_{M}$ were used as inputs. The output has a relative error ranging from +55\% to -35\% for temperatures between -20$^{\circ}$C and -10$^{\circ}$C, and +90\% to -47\% for temperatures below -40$^{\circ}$C. Particullarly, its main source of error is Z, where 3\% of error in Z can lead to a 12\% of error in ice water path (IWP), as observed by  \citet{saavedragarfiasAsymmetriesCloudMicrophysical2023}. 
Lastly,  $r_{ice}$ is retrieved troughout the Z-T relation \citep{griescheApplicationShipborneRemote2019}, where Z and T$_{M}$ were used as inputs. The output has 50\% of relative uncertainty. These uncertainties are quite high, highlighting the challenges of accurately retrieving ice properties.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:classification_algorithm}

\subsection{Cluster-based algorithm and cloud structure computation}\label{sec:new_method}

The cluster-based algorithm (CBA) is a novel algorithm for cloud classification. The CBA classifies clusters of hydrometeors based on their composition i.e the percentage of different hydrometeor inside the cluster. The algorithm also classifies clouds as single-layer or multi-layer based on time. Figure \ref{fig:classificationby_group_scheme} illustrate algorithm steps (numbered 1 to 7), which are described as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Cloud classification
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Cloud Mask: A cloud mask is generated from the CLOUDNET target classification
		\item Cluster Identification: Cloud pixels separated only by two pixels in any direction (analogous to 30 s - 1 min, and 20 - 50 m of height) are considered part of the same cloud. This is reached using a pixel dilation and contraction technique \citep{doughertyMathematicalMorphologyImage2018}, expanding and contracting each cluster (see Appendix \ref{sec:dilation} for a more detailed explanation of the method). In other words, clusters that are very close to each other will be merged
		\item Cloud Criteria: Whether the cluster has more than 100 pixels, it is considered a cloud (without considering Drizzle or rain pixels). Otherwise, it is deemed as noise and is excluded from further analysis. This number of pixels was selected to focus on clouds of significant size.
		\item Cloud Phase: Clouds are classified as liquid, ice, mixed-phase, precipitating liquid, precipitating ice or precipitating mixed-phase. The criteria of each cloud depends on the percentage of hydrometeors, and are defined as follow: 
		
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Liquid criteria: The percentage of Droplets and drizzle plus the percentage of Droplets must be greater than 70\%, i.e. P(Droplets and drizzle) + P(Droplets) > 70\%
			\item Ice criteria: The percentage of Ice must be greater than 90\%, i.e. P(Ice) > 90\% or the percentage of Droplets plus Ice \& droplets must be less than 10\%, i.e P(Droplets) + P(Ice \& droplets) < 10\%. These thresholds were defined empirically after an extensive verification of case studies. We observed that thunderstorm clouds are practically made of Ice and Ice \& droplets in most cases. However, they also have melting layer pixels, preventing the ice percentage from reaching 90\%. That's why we also verify if there is a small percentage of water inside these clouds. Otherwise, cloud phase is classified as mixed-phase.
		\end{enumerate}
		\item Rain criteria: If the cloud has more than 10 pixels of Drizzle or rain, it is considered as a precipitating cloud.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\item Multi-layer classification
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Drizzle Removal: For precipitating Ice clouds and precipitating Mixed-phase clouds, Drizzle pixels are removed below the melting layer. Below this layer, they are considered as rain pixels, thus they are removed to avoid using these pixels for cloud base height (CBH) estimations.
		\item Cloud Layer Criteria: For cloudy periods, time intervals where clouds overlap are categorized as multi-layer. Otherwise, clouds are categorized as a single-layer type. The minimum vertical distance between clouds to be considered as multilayer is one pixel. It was settled to avoid any possibility of considering multi-layers in further analysis.
		
	\end{enumerate}
	
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=.9\textwidth]{./figures/new_cba_scheme}
	\caption{Flowchart of the cluster-based algorithm (CBA). Details of each step are provided in the text.}
	\label{fig:classificationby_group_scheme}
\end{figure}

For single layer clouds, cloud structure such as cloud base and top height (CBH, CTH), and cloud thickness are calculated as follows: CBH and CTH are calculated as the first and last pixel within the cloud column. For non-precipitating cloud columns, the first pixel is detected by the ceilometer and the last by the CDR. For precipitating Ice or precipitating Mixed-phase clouds, the first pixel detected by the ceilometer is the Droplets or Drizzle \& droplets, which are within the rain layer. These pixels are probably rain pixels, that's why we remove them below the melting layer rather than using the CBH product directly from Cloudnet. Lastly, cloud thickness was calculated as the difference between cloud base and top height pixels.

Multi-layer clouds are problematic due to inaccurate LWP partitioning among cloud layers \citep{shupeDerivingArcticCloud2015}. The partitioning needs an accurate cloud profile, which cannot be assessed with only radar reflectivity. The reflectivity is not sensible to small cloud droplets and the lidar signal is totally attenuated by the first layer. Hence, liquid layers will be poorly identified causing an inaccurate determination of liquid and mixed phase clouds \citep{nomokonovaStatisticsCloudsTheir2019}. Therefore, the following analyses are focused only on single-layer clouds, since their physical properties can be accurately retrieved.

\subsection{Cloud typing assessment}\label{sec:daily_cycle}

A comparison between CBA and the profile-based algorithm (PBA) (see Appendix \ref{sec:old_method} for algorithm description), used by previous studies, is conducted in order to evaluate its applicability. 
Figure \ref{fig:cloud_classification_comparison} presents a comparison between PBA and CBA applied for a mixed phase on 6 May 2023 in Granada. The cloud phase can be checked in Figure \ref{fig:cloud_classification_comparison}.a, which shows a clear layer of mixed phase cloud. Below that, Figure \ref{fig:cloud_classification_comparison}.b shows that the PBA fragments the cloud structure into different types. It is easy to observe that the same cloud is being classified as ice or mixed-phase cloud multiple times in the PBA. On the other hand, Figure \ref{fig:cloud_classification_comparison}.c shows that the CBA preserves the homogeneity of the cloud and classifies it correctly during the entire cloudy time.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{./figures/scheme_cloud_classification.png}
	\caption{Comparison of cloud typing from different cloud classification algorithms at the AGORA station, on 6 May 2023; (a) HC from Cloudnet, indicating a clear single layer of Mixed-Phase cloud (b) PBA cloud typing, showing an inhomogeneous cloud classification. (c) CBA cloud typing, showing an homogeneous cloud classification.}
	\label{fig:cloud_classification_comparison}
\end{figure}

The inhomogeneous cloud classification of the PBA may result in assigning similar physical properties to different cloud types. As can be clearly seen in Figure~\ref{fig:cloud_classification_comparison}.b, both ice and mixed phase clouds will have similar daily cloud occurrence and average CBH. However, this is a consequence of the classification algorithm, which could affect further analysis of cloud type properties, reaching misleading conclusions.

To assess the impact of the cloud classification algorithm on cloud property statistics, Pearson correlation coefficients between cloud types were calculated based on their daily occurrence. Figure \ref{tab:pearson_correlation}.a and Figure \ref{tab:pearson_correlation}.b show only the relevant correlations we found for PBA and CBA, respectively. They are correlations between Mixed-Phase and Ice clouds, Liquid and Precipitating Liquid clouds, and Precipitating Mixed-Phase and Precipitating-Liquid clouds.

For the PBA, the correlations are 0.39 between ice and mixed-phase clouds and 0.32 between liquid and precipitating liquid. On the other hand, the corresponding correlations for the CBA are 0.09 and 0.19, respectively. As shown, the CBA exhibits weaker or negligible correlations, whereas those from the PBA are more pronounced. The pronounced correlations from PBA are related to its classification scheme, which may link clouds between each other. However, the CBA provides a time-homogenized cloud classification, avoiding the cloud linking. These findings underscore how these two algorithms associate different cloud types, which can potentially impact cloud property statistics.

Table \ref{tab:pearson_correlation} shows the correlation of daily average IWP between ice and mixed-phase clouds, for PBA and CBA, respectively. Their corresponding Pearson correlation coefficient (R) are 0.7 and 0.24, almost 3 times higher for PBA. Since there is no evidence that the IWP should correlate between Ice and Mixed-Phase clouds, there is no explanation for such high correlations observed for the PBA. Therefore, this algorithm can generate not explained relations between clouds. In this light, the new approach shows a promising classification method and will be employed in this study.
