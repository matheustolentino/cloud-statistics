\section{Results and discussion}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Annual Cloud Variability}\label{sec:anual_variability}

Figure \ref{fig:cloud_available}.a presents the monthly occurrence frequency at AGORA station, for different sky conditions, i.e. single-layer clouds, multi-layer clouds, clear sky, and noise (as defined in section \ref{sec:new_method}). The data reveal a distinct seasonal pattern in cloud cover and clear sky occurrences. Clear sky conditions (green line) are predominant most of the year, being most frequent in the summer, peaking in July and August (> 80\%), with a significant decline in spring, reaching the lowest point in March and April (40\%), when cloudy skies are predominant. This pattern indicates a higher prevalence of clear skies during the warmer months and increased cloudiness during the colder months.
Single-layer (blue line) and multi-layer clouds (orange line) exhibit very similar values with a relatively weak seasonal pattern throughout the year showing maxima in spring and minima in the summer months. Multi-layer clouds present a slightly more pronounced seasonal variation than single-layer clouds, with higher occurrence in spring (35\% for multi-layer and 25\% for single-layer clouds in April) and lower occurrence in summer (3\% for multi-layer versus 9\% for single-layer clouds in July). The noise category (red line) presents the minimum values and maintains a weak seasonal pattern throughout the year.

As aforementioned in section \ref{sec:new_method}, only single-layer clouds will be analyzed. Thus, the monthly occurrence of single layer clouds per cloud type is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:cloud_available}.b. The percentage of occurrence in this figure is calculated in relation to the total number of data. In other words, the sum of cloud occurrences per month in Figure \ref{fig:cloud_available}.b is the blue curve in Figure \ref{fig:cloud_available}.a, which is the single layer frequency in relation to the total number of data. 

Liquid clouds (blue) have quite small occurrence, with less than 3\% in March and Nov-Dec, when its frequency is higher. In summer, this cloud has almost zero occurrence (July-Aug), marking an ordinary seasonal trend. Precipitating liquid clouds have less than 2\% of occurrence throughout the year, but rises to 4\% in November. Clearly, precipitation from liquid clouds is maxima during Fall. This is the only season where these clouds surpass the occurrence of liquid clouds; however, their seasonality is ordinary.

Ice clouds (cyan) occurrence is varying around 5\% and 7\% throughout the year, except in July, when values sharply drop below 2\%. It marks a clear seasonal pattern, with a small presence of ice clouds in summer. Precipitating Ice clouds (gray) have maximum frequency during spring (8\%) and winter (5\%). Its frequency of occurrence sharply decreases in May, reaching their minimum in July, with less than 1\% of occurrence. This reveals a strong seasonality for liquid precipitation from ice clouds. Their maximum occurrence is remarkably in spring, decreasing just-after, with lower occurrence than ice clouds for the remainder period.

Mixed-phase clouds (yellow) exhibit a consistent occurrence around 3\% and 4\% throughout the year. Its steady frequency indicates an absence of seasonal pattern. Precipitating Mixed-phase clouds (orange) have peaks in April (6\%) and December (7\%). Their minimum is also reached in summer, being less than 2\% in June and August. This indicates a pronounced seasonality for liquid precipitation from mixed-phase clouds. Their occurrence exceeds those of Mixed-phase clouds only in spring and winter.

At the AGORA station, cloud frequency reaches a minimum during summer, when atmospheric stability is pronounced \citep{bedoya-velasquezSeasonalAnalysisAtmosphere2019}. Particularly, precipitating clouds are more affected due to their strong dependence on atmospheric instability. That's why the strongest seasonal patterns were observed for these clouds. Ice clouds are the only non-precipitating cloud with a pronounced seasonal pattern. In addition, precipitating clouds with strong seasonal variation were those with considerable amount of ice, i.e. Precipitating Ice and Precipitating Mixed-phase clouds. Consequently, precipitation in Granada is highly correlated to these clouds, which are more frequent in Spring. On the other hand, precipitation from Liquid clouds is more frequent in November (Fall). 

Despite the high atmospheric stability in summer, a few cloud instances are still being observed. During this season, Andalusia experiences easterly winds that transport moisture from the Mediterranean Sea \citep{bello-millanSimulationsTornadicSupercell2024}. If air masses are lifted enough to overcome the convective inhibition energy (CINE), conditions become favorable for cloud formation with substantial vertical development. Since temperatures are high in summer, air masses should be lifted to altitudes higher than other seasons. 

The analysis of the diurnal cycles revealed no clear diurnal patterns, thus no analysis on diurnal cycle is shown.

\subsection{Seasonal analysis of cloud macrophysics properties}

Cloud macrophysics are structure properties such as CBH, CTH and cloud thickness. Their seasonal evolution is displayed in Figure \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}. Each color represents a cloud type, where liquid clouds are in blue, ice clouds are in gray and mixed-phase clouds are in orange. Also, the lightest colors represent non precipitating clouds, and the darkest colors represent precipitation clouds (see legend on top of the figure). The values in parenthesis are the number of measurements of non-precipitating clouds (On the left) and precipitating clouds (On the right). Table \ref{tab:cloud_data} resumes the median values of CBH and cloud thickness observed in this figure.

Liquid clouds generally exhibit very low CBH, except in summer, which shows an atypical distribution with fewer data points (4,615) than other seasons. Unusually, this season presented high CBH values, with a median of 2.09\,km and few cases above the 75th percentile ($\sim$3.5 km). On the other hand, other seasons presented a 75th percentile of CBH around 2\,km, and median CBH varying from 1.31 (winter) to 1.49 (spring)\,km, as can be seen in Table \ref{tab:cloud_data}. The CTH exhibits the same seasonal variability as CBH but slightly higher. Thus, a narrow distribution of cloud thickness (Fig. \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}.c – zoomed for better visualization) is observed. Precipitating liquid clouds have median CBHs below 1\,km, except in summer, with 1.22\,km (see Tab. \ref{tab:cloud_data}). They exhibited a narrower distribution of CBH and CTH compared to liquid clouds. However, these clouds demonstrated higher cloud thickness, which is twice the width of that observed in liquid clouds during Summer-Winter, as shown in Table \ref{tab:cloud_data}.

Ice clouds show very high CBH, with the 25th percentile above 6\,km. Their median CBH is spanning from 7.24 (Winter) to 8.14 km (Fall) (see Tab. \ref{tab:cloud_data}).The 25/50/75th percentiles of these clouds remain relatively stable across seasons, indicating a well-defined structure that does not exhibit significant seasonal variation. The same pattern was observed for their CTH, which the 75th percentile exceeds 9 km (Fig. \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}.e), regardless of the station. Figure \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}.f shows that there are Ice clouds with more than 3\,km of width. However their median widths fluctuate around 1\,km, with maximum in spring (1.27\,km), as can be seen in Table \ref{tab:cloud_data}. Precipitating Ice clouds have low CBHs, with the 25th percentile below 2 km, except in summer when it is around 3\,km. The median CBH in winter and spring is consistent at 1.6 km. In contrast, during summer and fall, the median CBH increases to 3.46 km and 2.53 km, respectively. This variation indicates a pronounced seasonality. Figure \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}.f shows that these clouds have a broader distribution of Cloud thickness. The median values range from 3 to 4 km, with the lowest in winter (3.07\,km) and the highest in summer (3.96\,km). This indicates that these clouds are quite thick and exhibit a weak seasonal variability.

Mixed-phase clouds have CBHs at mid-level altitudes, with distributions that vary significantly across seasons (Fig. \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}.g). Seasonal variability is more pronounced in the 25th percentile, while the median CBH exhibits moderate variation. The lowest CBH occurs in winter (4.77 km), spring and summer have similar values (5.31 and 5.49 km, respectively), and fall presents the highest values at 6.50\,km. Although median values are high in fall, only summer has no CBH below 4\,km. Also, summer presented a significant narrow distribution of CBH. The same pattern was observed for CTH, showing a strong seasonal pattern in terms of CBH and CTH. However, median cloud thickness remains relatively stable around 0.67 km, except in summer, slightly rising to 0.78 km. Precipitating Mixed-phase clouds have low CBHs, with median below 2 km, except in summer, when it is 4.47\,km. This station showed minimal cloud presence below 2 km, with the 25th percentile exceeding 3 km. Differently, other seasons have clouds nearer to the ground, with median CBH values between 1 and 2 km (see Tab. \ref{tab:cloud_data}). The same pattern was observed for CTH, which showed high values in summer. It shows a strong seasonality for CBH and CTH. In terms of cloud thickness, values are $\sim$0.5 km thicker than other seasons (see Tab. \ref{tab:cloud_data}), also showing a strong seasonality in function of this variable.

Generally, precipitating clouds have much lower CBH than non-precipitating clouds of the same type, and show a stronger seasonal pattern, with higher levels in summer. 
It is important to note that, despite Cloudnet post-processed data being corrected for liquid attenuation, cloud tops for precipitating clouds can be underestimated due to high liquid attenuation in some particular cases. In addition, for precipitating liquid clouds, CBH is sometimes determined by "Drizzle \& Droplets" pixels. It is hard to know whether these pixels are falling or not when they are inside a “Drizzle or rain” layer. In such cases, the cloud base could be underestimated. At the moment, recent studies are trying to overcome this problem, which can be more intense on marine sites \citep{roschkeDiscriminatingDrizzleRain2024}, however, it is rarely observed at the AGORA station. In contrast, this is most common for precipitating ice and mixed-phase clouds, where we do not use these pixels to define the cloud base. Instead, we rely on those within the melting layer, as outlined in the methods section.

\subsection{Cloud microphysical properties}\label{sec:cloud_microphys}

In this section we investigate the inter-annual variability of microphysical retrievals for each category of single-layer clouds detected at the AGORA station. Liquid water content (LWC) and droplet effective radius ($r_{liq}$) were evaluated for Liquid and Mixed-Phase clouds. Ice water content (IWC) and ice effective radius ($r_{ice}$) were evaluated for Ice and Mixed-Phase clouds. The analysis was performed for the precipitating case and non-precipitating case.

\subsubsection{Liquid clouds}

For liquid clouds, Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.c shows the median seasonal profile of LWC on the left, the monthly median profiles of LWC on top-right panel, and the monthly boxplots of LWP on the bottom-right panel. As can be seen on the left panel, Liquid clouds generally have LWC below 250 mg m$^{-3}$. However, 3 peaks with notably higher values are identified in winter, spring and summer. It shows that for winter and summer, a few clouds with CBHs between 3-4 km contain more water than the low-level clouds. Especially in winter, where more than 1000 mg m$^{-3}$ were found just after 3\,km. For spring, a time series analysis (not shown) revealed that high LWC at low levels (below 500\,m) were found during spring of 2020, 2021 and 2022. In spring, the monthly median profiles of LWC in the top-right panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.a shows quite large LWC at low-levels in March and May. In winter, high LWC values were found in January and February, while summer presented peaks in June and August. The bottom-right panel shows quite high mean LWP (black star) in April and October. Although the median (red line) stays stable, the average is much higher, highlighting a wide variety of liquid clouds these months. From January to April, and August to October, there is a gradual increase in mean LWP, while the median does not change. This trend corresponds with the increased occurrence of liquid clouds from summer to fall, impacted by different mechanisms of cloud formation.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.c shows the same cloud properties statistics (LWC and LWP) as shown in Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.c, but for Precipitating Liquid clouds. Note that we avoid the rain contribution in LWP integrating the LWC inside the cloud.
The left panel shows that spring and summer have the largest median LWC at low-levels. Especially during summer, which presented almost 2000 mg m$^{-3}$ at $\sim$ 200 m. Spring showed a minor peak even closer to the ground, and a second one at $\sim$ 400 m.
This indicates that some rain droplets are being mistakenly classified as part of the cloud base. As aforementioned, distinguishing between Drizzle \& droplets and Drizzle or rain is challenging for this particular cloud, and some of these pixels (Drizzle \& droplets) actually represent rain. Thus, large droplets are attributed to the cloud base, increasing the LWC at low-levels, as observed. Above this level, an increasing trend in LWC up to 2 km is observed for summer, fall, and winter, except in spring. Spring and winter show a peak of $\sim$700 mg m$^{-3}$ around 2.7 and 2.2\,km, and spring and fall present LWC over 3\,km of height. The monthly profiles on the top-right panel shows that the peak of LWC in summer is due to high values in June (> 1000 mg m$^{-3}$). The LWC peaks in spring are primarily found in April, and the peak observed in winter is located in January. In addition, large values of LWC are also observed in October and November (fall), just after 2\,km of height. The bottom panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.c shows the monthly boxplots of LWP, where the 25/75th percentiles show larger variability in LWP in January, suggesting a wide variety of liquid clouds during this month. In contrast, November shows low variability while also having the highest number of registered cloudy profiles (blue line).
Spring exhibits consistent LWP across its months, with median below 50 g m$^{-2}$. Summer shows even less LWP, except for June, as previously observed.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

For liquid clouds, the left panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.b shows that the seasonal median profiles of $r_{liq}$ have no significant differences. We observed a sharp increase in droplet size within the first 500 meters, followed by a gradual increase up to 3\,km, and then fluctuations around $\sim$6 $\mu$m at higher altitudes. It suggests a consistent droplet size throughout different atmospheric conditions. These findings underscore the stable nature of liquid clouds in terms of $r_{liq}$.The top-right panel displays monthly median profiles of $r_{liq}$, showing that droplet sizes mostly ranges between 3 and 8 $\mu$m. Smaller sizes are observed near the ground, with larger sizes at higher altitudes, particularly in November, where higher values are seen above 2\,km. The bottom panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.c shows the monthly distribution of $r_{liq}$ (violin plots), where the median is stable around 5 $\mu$m, with a small decrease in July. Although July has the fewest recorded cloudy profiles (blue line), our dataset is robust and indicates that the frequency of these clouds is indeed very low during this month. Consequently, these limited cloud observations can still effectively represent the general microphysics of these clouds

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The median seasonal profiles of $r_{liq}$(see left panel of Fig. \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.d) for Precipitating Liquid clouds shows significant differences. Spring and summer have similar $r_{liq}$ below 1\,km, fluctuating around 5 $\mu$m. Above this level, droplet radius increases with highest rate in summers up to 2\,km, while spring keeps $r_{liq} $ roughly around 5 $\mu$m until 3km, followed by sharp increase to 20 $\mu$m. Winter has the second largest $r_{liq} $ at the first 2 km ($\thicksim$8 $\mu$m), disregarding large rain drizzle particles close to the ground. These particles cause sharp peaks near the surface in summer, fall and winter. In addition, $r_{liq} $ in fall are the largest (below 3 km), reaching 15 $\mu$m around 1 km. The monthly median profiles of $r_{liq}$in the top-right panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.d shows that larger sizes in fall occur in November. Larger sizes related to the first peak below 500 m are associated with Drizzle \& droplets pixels linked to rain, as previously observed. The monthly distribution of $r_{liq}$ in the bottom-right panel shows larger sizes in November, as already observed. During spring and early summer, the median is maintained around 10 $\mu$m, then decreases in summer, and increases again in fall, when the number of recorded cloudy profiles is maximum.

Liquid clouds generally do not show seasonal patterns in LWC, except in two altitude ranges: 0–1 km and 3–4 km. In the 0–1 km range, a peak of LWC appears in spring, while in the 3–4 km range, LWC peaks in summer, with a slightly lower peak in winter. For $r_{liq}$, no seasonal pattern was found, and its values are generally around 5 $\mu$m.
For precipitating liquid clouds, both LWC and $r_{liq}$ are larger than those for Liquid clouds and exhibit a clear seasonal pattern. These properties were influenced by Drizzle \& droplets pixels at low levels, as previously discussed, which increases the uncertainty of LWC and $r_{liq}$ near the ground. \subsubsection{Mixed Phase clouds}

For Mixed-Phase clouds, Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed} presents different panels from the previous figures to account for the presence of both water and ice. Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.a-b display the seasonal and monthly statistics of LWC and $r_{liq}$, while Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.c-d present the corresponding statistics for IWC and $r_{ice}$. Precipitating mixed-phase clouds will be analyzed right after.  

Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.a shows the median seasonal profile of LWC on the left, the monthly median profiles of LWC on top-right panel, and the monthly boxplots of LWP on the bottom-right panel. As can be seen on the left panel, the median seasonal LWC shows large variability below 4\,km and above 10 km. Below 4 km, all seasons exhibit an LWC peak of nearly 200 mg m$^{-3}$ near the ground ($\sim$700\,m), except in winter, where this peak is around 400 mg m$^{-3}$. Then, one more peak was observed in spring ($\sim$2.8), and two more peaks were observed in fall ($\sim$2.5 and 3.3$\sim$ km of height), each exceeding 100 mg m$^{-3}$. The monthly median profiles of LWC in the top-right panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.a indicate that the second peak in spring occurs in May. The two fall peaks are likely due to October and November, with values around 150–200 mg m$^{-3}$ at that altitude. The bottom-right panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.a presents monthly boxplots of LWP, showing that January has the highest mean LWP and the largest difference from the median. This month contributes to the prominent near-ground peak observed in the winter profile. Overall, monthly median LWP (red line) are relatively stable compared to the mean. It indicates a wide variety of Mixed-Phase clouds occurring at different altitudes, as seen in the distinct seasonal peaks.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.c shows the same as Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.a, but for IWC. The left panel shows the seasonal median IWC, where it increases from 2\,km up to 7 km for winter and spring, and up to 8 km for fall and summer. Winter and fall exhibit nearly identical behavior, with summer following a similar pattern to fall up to nearly 7 km. Above this level, summer has the largest amount of IWC, with a peak of 22 mg m$^{-3}$ around 8 km, and a second peak of 17 mg m$^{-3}$ around 11 km. The monthly median profiles of IWC in the top-right panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.c shows IWC of about 20-40 mg m$^{-3}$ between 4 and 10 km, except for July, which presented more elevated IWC above 10 km (more than 60 mg m$^{-3}$). Furthermore, the monthly boxplots of IWP in the bottom-right panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_IWC_DER_IER_mixed}.c present high differences between the mean and the median during spring, summer and winter. Typically, monthly median IWP are close to zero, being slightly larger in April and summer, when the mean fluctuates between 45-55 g m$^{-2}$. It shows the presence of a few Mixed-phase clouds in these months with larger content of ice.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The left panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.b shows the median seasonal profiles of $r_{liq}$ for Mixed-Phase clouds. This panel shows differences between fall and summer at 4\,-\,5 km, where fall presented a valley, while the other stations presented a peak. Except for fall, median $r_{liq}$ increases with height up to 5 km, where there is almost zero LWC (between 4 - 8\,km) as already observed (see Fig. \ref{fig:LWC_liq_and_pre_liq}.a, left). Summer presented the largest droplets between 4\,-\,5 km, with $\sim$ 15 $\mu$m of radius. Above 4 km, winter and spring profiles are similar, each with maximum IWC of approximately 25 mg m$^{-3}$ at 6 km. However, spring has an additional peak of similar magnitude around 12 km.
