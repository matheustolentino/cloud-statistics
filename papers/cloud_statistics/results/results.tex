\subsection{Annual variability and daily cycle of clouds}\label{sec:daily_cycle}

Since the cloud classification by hydrometers grouping showed a more comprehensive classification, this algorithm is employed in the following analysis. This section aims to analyze the inter-annual and daily cycle variability of cloud frequency of occurrence at the Granada station. Therefore, left panel of Figure \ref{fig:cloud_available} shows the monthly frequency of occurrence of different sky conditions, categorized into single-layer clouds, multi-layer clouds, clear sky, and noise. The data reveal a distinct seasonal pattern in cloud cover and clear sky occurrences. Clear sky conditions (green line) are predominant all year long, being most frequent in the summer months (80\%), peaking in July and August, with a significant decline in spring (40\%), reaching the lowest point in March and April. This pattern indicates a higher prevalence of clear skies during the warmer months and increased cloudiness during the colder months. 
Single-layer clouds (blue line) exhibit a relatively weak seasonal pattern throughout the year, with a slight peak in spring (35\%) and a decrease in the summer months (10\%), suggesting a moderate variation in single-layer cloud occurrences. Multi-layer clouds (orange line) show a similar pattern, with a peak in March and April and a reduction during the summer, indicating a seasonal preference for multi-layer clouds in the spring. The noise category (red line) maintains a low and stable occurrence throughout the year, with minor fluctuations. Overall, the data highlight clear seasonal variations in sky conditions, with clear skies dominating in summer and increased cloudiness, both single and multi-layer, in the cooler months. Since this station exhibit a quit low cloud occurrence, a need to handle multilayer clouds is revealed to fill gaps of cloud properties at regions such as the western Mediterranean. Particularly, these multi-layer are problematic due to inaccurate LWP partitioning, leading a misclassification of liquid pixels inside clouds, as already discussed by \cite{tatiananomokonovaStatisticsCloudsTheir2019}. Therefore, the following analyses are focus only on single-layer clouds, seeing their physical properties can be retrieved more accurately.

Monthly frequency of occurrence is depicted in the right panel of Figure \ref{fig:cloud_available}. Mixed-phase clouds (yellow) exhibit relatively consistent occurrences with slight fluctuations around 3\% and 4\%. Liquid-precipitable (dark blue) has a modest variation with zero occurrence in summer, followed by an increase during the transition to winter, reaching a maximum of 4\% in November, suggesting increased precipitation during fall. Liquid clouds (blue) are more variable, showing a percentage of 3\% in March and December, against zero in summer. Ice clouds (cyan) drops sharply in July and maintain almost constant levels around 5\% and 7\% throughout the rest of the year, indicating hotter temperatures that inhibit ice cloud formation during summer. Ice-precipitable clouds (gray) have a notable peaks of 8\% in spring, followed by a decrease in summer (1\%) and an increase in fall, until reach a local maximum of 5\% during winter. It suggest a seasonal variations in ice-related precipitation with maximum in spring, unlike the case of water-related precipitation, which showed a maximum in November. Mixed-phase-precipitable clouds (orange) peak in April (6\%) and December (7\%), indicating higher chances of mixed-phase precipitation towards spring and the end of the year. Overall, ice, ice-precipitable, and mixed-phase clouds have the strongest seasonal pattern, indicating a higher sensibility of ice due to thermodynamic conditions than liquid droplets. Moreover, the precipitation in this region is intrinsic related to ice and mixed phase clouds i.e. with low temperatures.

An important mechanism associated with temperature decreases and responsible for extreme rainfall in Eastern Spain is the occurrence of cut-off lows (COLs). COLs account for nearly all rain events in Valencia, Spain \citep{ferreiraCutOffLowsExtreme2021}, and are likely to affect the Granada station as well. This influence is attributed to the easterly wind component of COLs formed in the warm Mediterranean \citep{porcuStudyCutoffLow2007}, which can reach the station, bringing humidity and cold air masses that favor cloud formation.
In Valencia, \cite{ferreiraCutOffLowsExtreme2021} reported the rainiest days occurring in winter, with extreme rainfall events in spring and fall, aligning with our observations. During the summer, although almost non-precipitating clouds were observed at our station, temperatures were higher. Additionally, the Iberian Peninsula experiences a large low-pressure area over northern Africa, with high-pressure systems over the Atlantic Ocean and eastern and central Europe. This results in easterly winds over Andalusia, transporting moisture from the Mediterranean Sea \citep{f.j.bello-millanSimulationsTornadicSupercell2024}. This combination of high temperatures and humidity can create an environment conducive to storm formation. In this context, the few clouds observed in summer are likely related to convectively formed cumulonimbus clouds. This will be more evident when study their physical properties in further sections.

Next, the daily cycle of cloud occurrence should uncover the influence of temperature around mid-day, when the solar radiance is more intense. Although we already discuss the influence of synoptic and local phenomena in cloud formation, convection clouds should appear in the diurnal cycle. From Figure \ref{fig:cloud_occurence_daily_cycle}, its clear that there is no convection for any type of cloud during summer, fall and winter. Despite that fall shows an increase of ice clouds around mid-day, they are probably cirrus clouds, since their median base height and thickness are 8.5\,km and 1.5\,km, respectively (see Figure \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}).

However, spring shows a possible presence of convective clouds, with a slight peak of occurrence for Ice-precipitable and Mixed-phase-precipitable clouds between 09:00 and 21:00 UTC. Different from non-precipitating clouds which reached a minimum around mid-day, these clouds doubles the occurrence from 4\% at 00:00 to 8\% at 16:00-17:00 UTC. These observations are consistent with the findings of \cite{shahiAssessmentSpatiotemporalVariability2022}, where convection-permitting models of the Iberian Peninsula (IP) captured high values of mean precipitation during spring. Additionally, \cite{alexandrerios-entenzaMoistureRecyclingIberian2014} revealed that specific synoptic conditions, such as a reduction in the strength of the zonal circulation, can favor local and mesoscale convection during spring in the western region of the IP. This analysis confirms that the maximum cloud and rain occurrence observed during peak sunlight hours may be strongly associated with local convection.

\subsection{Seasonal patterns in cloud base, top, and thickness}

Seasonal evolution of cloud base height, top height and thickness heights are displayed in Figure \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}. This figure shows that precipitating clouds exhibit a significantly lower cloud base height (top panel) compared to non-precipitating clouds, as well as a narrower distribution. Regarding non-precipitating ice clouds, they possess the highest cloud base heights, with median values around 8\,km. These high ice clouds with small ice water path (Figure \ref{fig:IWC_ice}) can be associated to cirrus clouds. Next, non-precipitating mixed-phase clouds are the second highest cloud type we found, which show considerable variability in height except during summer, when values stabilize at 6 km. Differently, cloud base is high in fall, where the 50th percentile reach a maximum of 7 km of height, along with high variability. On the other hand, non-precipitating liquid clouds showed higher cloud base variability during summer, achieving significantly different heights compared with other seasons. Overall, in summer, liquid clouds, precipitating mixed-phase clouds, and ice precipitating clouds demonstrate elevated cloud base heights, attributed to the relatively high temperatures and low humidity at this season. Specifically, liquid clouds display the highest cloud base variability during summer, with the 50th and 75th percentiles around 4 km and 6 km, respectively. These clouds can be related to stratocumulus and stratus clouds, which were dis-coupled from cumulonimbus at final stage. 

As observed for the cloud base, precipitating clouds have a significantly lower median cloud top height (middle panel of Figure \ref{fig:cloud_macrophys}) compared to non-precipitating clouds and exhibit a narrower cloud top distribution, except in the case of ice clouds. Non-precipitating ice clouds maintain a similar distribution across seasons, highlighting another feature that suggest the presence of cirrus clouds, while ice-precipitable clouds demonstrate highly variable cloud tops. Given their well-defined cloud bases, these clouds exhibit greater cloud thickness, indicative of storm activity at the station.
The cloud tops for liquid clouds mirror the variability of their cloud bases, particularly during summer. These clouds exhibit a well-defined cloud thickness, with purely liquid clouds having a thickness below 1 km and liquid-precipitable clouds reaching up to 1 km in fall, as indicated by the 75th percentile. This aligns with the observed peak occurrence of these clouds in this season.
Mixed-phase clouds also follow the behavior of their cloud bases and display a well-defined cloud thickness. Generally, non-precipitating mixed-phase clouds have a thickness around 500 m, increasing to 1 km for precipitating mixed-phase clouds, with slightly higher values in summer. For non-precipitating mixed-phase clouds, median cloud tops vary around 7 km during spring, summer, and winter, but reach 8.5 km in fall. Precipitating mixed-phase clouds have cloud tops around 3 km in spring, fall, and winter, peaking at 7 km in summer.

\subsection{Cloud microphysics properties}

\subsubsection{Liquid clouds}

Liquid clouds shows high variability of LWC in function of the height. The domain with elevated LWC is marked by hot colors and white lines, which marks constant values of 1000 mg m$^{-3}$ (Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq}). These high values represent just a few observations, and are hidden in seasonal median profiles above 2 km on the left panel. Only values at low levels during spring were clearly seen by pronounced peaks surpassing 250 mg m$^{-3}$. Overall, during colder months (winter and spring), LWC for liquid clouds can vary from 50 to 1000 mg m$^{-3}$. Onthe other hand, LWC in summer is sparse but can be found at higher levels as expected. Warm months at this region has high temperature and less relative humidity \citep{XXX}. 
Next, average liquid water path (LWP) (bottom panel of Figure \ref{fig:LWC_liq}) demonstrates that from January to June, and also early fall, there is a considerable variability in LWP, highlighting how different liquid clouds can be in these months. It is clearly observed by differences between the average (marked as red line), and the median (marked as the black star). A notable increase in the median LWP occurs in April, aligning with their maximum cloud occurrence. From August to October, there is a gradual increase in LWP, with a noticeable difference between the median and the mean. This trend corresponds with the increased occurrence of liquid clouds in fall, which is impacted by different mechanisms. Since we reported a few occurrences of these clouds, their statistics will be much more sensible to these mechanisms.

Seasonal median profiles of LWC show high variability with height and a pronounced peak around 300 meters in spring. This variability revels that there is no significant difference of the amount of water in liquid clouds between seasons. In general, the magnitude of the LWC is relatively small, and values are fluctuating around 70 mg m$^{-3}$ in the entire vertical, except about 300 meters. It is not shown here, but at this level, the median values are strong influenced by liquid clouds with high amounts of water in spring 2020 \cite{XXX}. Undoubtedly, the modest frequency of occurrence of this cloud category makes their microphysics quite variable as already observed. Next, although LWP in summer are the smallest, there are some peaks identified at 2 and 5 km. This season presented no considerable difference between the average and median LWP (bottom panel) except for June, showing that these values are representing just a few occurrences of this cloud, which can be checked by the blue line. This line shows the number of cloud profiles used to calculate the statistics per months.

In terms of droplet effective radius ($ r_{liq} $), a totally different nature is observed in relation to LWC. The last has expressive amounts of LWC at low and high layers (< 4 km and > 10 km), while the first is exhibiting high droplet sizes at middle levels. Large values of droplet sizes can be observed on top panel of Figure \ref{fig:DER_mixed} in dark blues and hot tones. As already discussed, liquid water are not common at those levels, but when detected, are followed by high mass concentration, suggesting the detection of cumulus clouds. Overall, summer presents higher droplets compared to colder months, with effective radius between 14-22 $\mu$m at medium heights (> 4 km and < 6 km), and between 12-18$\mu$m around 10 km. On the other hand, droplet size are generally close to 10 $\mu$m inside these clouds. This is shown by the monthly distribution of $ r_{liq} $, where the mode are located at even small values. In fact, cloud droplets are slight larger in summer and has good probability to reach 15 $ r_{liq} $ over the year, but in general they have more small droplets. 

In addition, there is a clear dependency of droplets size with the height. Seasonal profiles on the left panel of Figure \ref{fig:DER_mixed} shows droplet size increasing up to 5 km, then starting to decrease. However, the seasonal profiles highlights a general behavior, which can be explained by the evaporation of liquid cloud droplets trough air particles injection into clouds. The injection of particles will increase the water vapor competition, evaporating some droplets while ice particles are also formed due to low temperatures. This factors will decrease the size of water droplets and increase the mass concentration of ice particles. The last can be confirmed by left panel of Figure \ref{fig:IER_mixed}.

The analysis of droplet effective radius ($ r_{liq} $) in cumulonimbus clouds reveals intriguing seasonal and altitude-dependent dynamics. Summer stands out with notably elevated $ r_{liq} $ values ranging up to 20 $\mu$m from ground level to 3 km altitude, attributed to the presence of large droplets typical in cumulonimbus clouds. These findings underscore the impact of strong liquid attenuation on $ r_{liq} $ measurements, complicating accurate retrieval methods. Boxplot analysis highlights stable median $ r_{liq} $ values around 10 $\mu$m, yet exhibits significant monthly variability, peaking above 20 $\mu$m in winter and spring, and surpassing 30 $\mu$m in summer. These insights emphasize the need for refined observational techniques to accurately capture and interpret $ r_{liq} $ in varying meteorological conditions.

\subsubsection{Ice Clouds}

Our analysis of ice cloud liquid water content (IWC) reveals distinct seasonal and altitudinal variations. The majority of ice clouds have bases around 6 to 8 km; however, the contour plots indicate significant IWC values predominantly between 4 and 8 km, except for January when extremely high values were detected as low as 2 km. This anomaly in January is attributed to two extreme weather events: the Filomena storm in 2020 and the Gloria storm in 2021, which significantly impacted the western Mediterranean region \cite{martadealfonsoStormGloriaSea2021, enriquepravia-sarabiaRoleAerosolConcentration2023, marcosgarciasotilloEvaluationOperationalCMEMS2021}. Ice clouds at altitudes of 4 to 8 km typically exhibit IWC ranging from 100 to 300 mg m$^{-3}$ during winter, the transition to summer, and fall. Boxplot analyses of the liquid water path (LWP) show the highest variability in January due to these extreme snow events, which skew the statistics, usually characterized by low median IWC values. Mean IWC values generally hover around 50 mg m$^{-3}$ in spring, fall, and winter, influenced heavily by the Filomena and Gloria storms, while late summer values approach zero, with almost no ice clouds observed during this season. Seasonal profiles further support these findings, showing consistent IWC values between 4 and 8 km during spring, early summer, and fall, with peaks around 200 mg m$^{-3}$. Notably, early winter profiles reveal a significant peak exceeding 500 mg m$^{-3}$ at 2 km, corroborating the impact of the extreme snow events observed in January

Our investigation into precipitating ice clouds highlights significant ice water content (IWC) between 2 and 6 km throughout the year, with values ranging from 100 to 700 mg m$^{-3}$. These clouds contain the largest amount of ice compared to other cloud types, with particularly high IWC during winter, summer, and early fall. In summer, IWC values are found at slightly higher altitudes, consistent with the median cloud base located at 4 km. Notably, July exhibits the highest IWC, reaching 1000 mg m$^{-3}$ at 9 km, despite the minimal occurrence of these clouds, as observed from a 5-year dataset.

Boxplot analysis of liquid water path (LWP) indicates that IWC is relatively high in these clouds year-round due to their high thickness and small portion of liquid water. However, the substantially higher summer values create an impression of lower annual values. July, with significantly fewer occurrences than August, results in a higher average LWP in August. This pattern is also seen in September, where higher average LWP corresponds to fewer occurrences of high IWC clouds.

Seasonal profiles of IWC reveal distinct behaviors. Winter and spring exhibit similar vertical profiles with peaks at 4 km, with winter showing slightly higher values below this level, and nearly equal values above. Summer and fall also share similar profiles, both peaking at 6 km, though fall has higher values below this peak, and equal values above. Additionally, summer features a distinct peak at around 10 km, slightly higher than the maximum observed in July. This is due to averaging data over the entire summer, shifting the peak 1 km lower when considering the season as a whole.
